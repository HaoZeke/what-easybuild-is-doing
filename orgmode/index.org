#+TITLE: What EasyBuild Is Doing
#+OPTIONS: toc:nil num:nil

#+begin_export rst
.. rst-class:: eb-subtitle

An interactive book about EasyBuild: how it decides what to build, on a
laptop or on a national machine, and about the four things everyone gets
wrong about it.
#+end_export

EasyBuild's own documentation tells you what to type. It stays almost
silent on what happens when you do.

That silence is expensive. Reference documentation covers every
parameter, and a workshop series covers the exercises. Between them
they will get you to a working install.

Neither explains why an easyconfig is a Python file. Neither explains
why a toolchain is a hierarchy rather than a name, or why the version
number next to a dependency is not a minimum.

People learn those three by breaking a production software stack, which
is a slow and public way to learn anything.

So this is the other book.

* Start here

[[file:00-tutorial-read-an-easyconfig.org][Read an easyconfig the way
EasyBuild does]]. Twenty minutes, and nothing to install.

At the end you can predict what EasyBuild will download, name the
easyblock it will look for, and say which dependencies a toolchain
permits.

After that, either follow [[file:learning-path.org][the path]], which is
six levels of about an hour each with the prerequisites named, or pick by
what you are doing.

Reading straight through works too. Chapters 1 to 4 are the mechanisms
everything else rests on. Chapters 5 to 9 are the parameters that cost
the most time when misread, each built around a failure that actually
happened. Chapters 10 to 12 are the ones where you do something.
Chapters 13 to 19 are the machinery: the steps a build runs, what it is
allowed to see, and what comes out. Chapters 20 to 24 are the working
chapters, from reading a failure to writing a recipe, an easyblock and a
development environment. Chapters 25 and 26 are how a stack reaches a
machine that never built it, and chapter 27 is four failures in full.

* Where this runs

Not only on a supercomputer, and the book is careful about the
difference.

EasyBuild is a Python package. It installs with =pip= into a virtual
environment, needs a modules tool and a compiler, and builds software on
a laptop exactly as it does on a compute node. Several of the recordings
in this book were made that way, and their provenance says so: the
failing build in
[[file:20-reading-a-failed-build.org][chapter 20]] and the checks in
[[file:21-writing-one-from-nothing.org][chapter 21]] ran on a
workstation, with no cluster involved.

EESSI goes further. Its own installation instructions cover a laptop, and
CernVM-FS ships a client profile for exactly that case, so a machine with
no software stack of its own can mount one and load optimised modules in
minutes. [[file:26-the-same-stack-somewhere-else.org][Chapter 26]] does
it on four different kinds of machine.

What changes on a large shared machine is the *consequences*, not the
mechanism: many users, one tree, a scheduler, several
microarchitectures, and a rebuild that costs somebody a night. Where a
chapter is about those consequences it says so. Where it is about the
mechanism, the mechanism is the same on your own machine, and you can run
it there while reading.

* What you need to read this

Nothing, and that is the point.

EasyBuild's own tutorial opens by asking for Linux, Python, an
environment modules tool and a working EasyBuild install. That is a
reasonable ask of someone who has already decided to use EasyBuild, and
an unreasonable one of someone still deciding whether to.

Every sample here runs on your machine, in the page. Edit one and the
answer changes as you type.

Three of the four kinds work off tables exported from eb-stack at build
time rather than retyped. So a page cannot drift from the framework's
own data.

A cross-check compares the browser's answers against the binary's on
every build. A headless browser then checks that every sample on every
page is live and answering.

The fourth reads an easyconfig into its evaluated model, and it is a
reduced reader rather than the real parser. It follows single-line
assignments and *names what it skipped*. Where the real parser is
needed it says so instead of guessing.

That gets you a lot, and not everything. Nothing in a browser can
configure, compile or link, and nothing here pretends to.

Where a step needs a real toolchain you get a recording of a real run
instead, with where and when it ran printed beside it. That turns out
to be the right way to teach those steps. The operational skill is
reading the log rather than watching the progress bar.
